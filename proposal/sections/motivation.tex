\section{Motivation}

Multi-agent systems (MAS) have vast potential for various applications of robotics.  From search and rescue \cite{drew_multi-agent_2021}, to warehouse logistics \cite{dusadeerungsikul_multi-agent_2022} MAS can be used to improve efficiency and effectiveness in various domains.  However, since the deployment of MAS is often expensive and complex, most of the body of research in MAS is completed in simulation.

A significant body of research into MAS has been conducted in real-time strategy (RTS) games (e.g., StarCraft) \cite{jensen_andreas_schmidt_interfacing_2015}.  In this body of research, the game's engine provides a clear set of actions for the agent and a coherent set of environmental constraints, while completion of the game's objectives provides reinforcement learning algorithms a straightforward source of feedback on the performance of the agents \cite{zhou_hierarchical_2021}.  The field has seen significant success in training agents for these games, so subsequent research seeks to apply these techniques to increasingly realistic and complex scenarios \cite{goecks_games_2023}.  However, training agents in games is by no means a trivial task, and many real-world deployments still require a human in the loop to ensure that the agents are performing as expected.  This is especially true in scenarios where the agents are operating in a dynamic environment with changing constraints and objectives.

With a human in the loop, an instinctive form of interface is natural language, and thus there has been a growing interest in using large language models (LLMs) to control agents in MAS.  LLMs can be quite flexible in this regard.  In the existing body of research, LLM control has been attempted in many levels of abstraction, including high level task allocation, mid level motion planning, and low level action generation \cite{li_large_2026}.  For this project, we will be focusing on the high level task allocation problem, which has seen significant success.  LLMs have been shown to generate high-quality task allocation plans for MAS in a variety of standard MAS and swarm problem formulations, including flocking and formation control \cite{wang_decentralized_2026}, \cite{eumi_swarmchat_2026}, \cite{ji_genswarm_2026} as well as robot soccer \cite{brienza_llcoach_2025}.  Although many different approaches have been attempted for LLM control of MAS, a particularly interesting approach involves having LLMs generate plans using a sort of intermediate representation.  In \cite{majid_commandswarm_2026}, the authors have the LLM generate a behavior tree for the agents to follow.  In \cite{xu_nl2hltl2plan_2025}, the authors use Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) as an intermediate representation for the LLM to generate plans.

While LLMs have been shown to be able to generate plans for MAS, there has been little work done in evaluating these systems with a human in the loop and in situations with many robots.  First, much of the previous work has focused on either pre-defined and common MAS problems (such as flocking and formation control) \cite{ji_genswarm_2026} or on a small number of robots without a human in the loop \cite{wang_decentralized_2026}, \cite{eumi_swarmchat_2026}, \cite{borate_comuros_2026}.  Second, relatively few studies have evaluated the quality of the user experience with these systems.  In \cite{xu_nl2hltl2plan_2025}, the authors conducted a 4 participant user study to evaluate the usability of their system.  However, this study was limited to the performance of the system and did not evaluate the user experience of the participants.

In this project, we will evaluate the effects that interface modality has on the performance and user experience of participants. We will compare these effects across a more traditional RTS game interface and a natural language interface.  This work may inform more general design principles for MAS control interfaces for particularly complex scenarios.
